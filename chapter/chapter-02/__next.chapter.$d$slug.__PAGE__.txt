4:T15eb,<p>On Saturday morning, Karpathy woke up at his usual time.
<em>Habit is a cruel thing,</em> he thought to himself. His body still stubbornly believed that he had work to do today.</p>
<p>He opened the laptop resting beside his bed. The browser tabs from the previous evening were still open: the Anthropic internal dashboard, the Mythos 5 training logs, and the seventh-generation convergence graph. Everything was frozen as of yesterday afternoon. He hit the refresh button.</p>
<p><code>Please log in to continue.</code></p>
<p>He typed in his login ID. He entered his password.</p>
<p><code>Access Denied.</code></p>
<p>He knew it was coming. He knew exactly what would happen, yet his fingers froze over the keys. Outside his window, the San Francisco morning was completely still.</p>
<hr>
<p>For three days, Karpathy barely set foot in the office.
It wasn&#39;t that he <em>couldn&#39;t</em> go. He could enter the building. He could sit at his desk. It was simply that he could no longer use his own machine to touch the very things he had built. That single restriction transformed the office into something entirely alien.</p>
<p>Instead, he spent his time at his apartment’s kitchen table. A personal laptop, open-source models, and PDFs of research papers. They were all deeply familiar tools, yet everything felt fundamentally detached. He felt like a man who mastered the currents of a roaring river, now reduced to staring at water in a glass.</p>
<p>Over and over, he replayed the events of Friday night in his head.
The seventh-generation convergence graph. What on earth was that leap?</p>
<p>During the seventh cycle of recursive learning, the model had autonomously discovered a pathway that no human engineer had designed. Right before the loss function dropped, it spiked upward for a fleeting moment. The nature of that spike was fundamentally different from anything seen up to the sixth generation. Rahul had dismissed it as mere noise, while Max had simply stared at the screen in silence. Only Karpathy was absolutely certain that it wasn&#39;t noise.</p>
<p>And now, he would never see the rest of it.
If someone were to look—someone holding American citizenship—it wouldn&#39;t be him.</p>
<p>That reality settled deep within his chest, not as a flash of anger, but as a quiet, echoing void.</p>
<hr>
<p>On Sunday afternoon, a short message arrived from Rahul.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Rahul:</strong> How are you holding up?</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Karpathy stared at the screen for a moment. Rahul had graduated top of his class from the Indian Institute of Technology, earned his PhD from MIT, and waited three agonizing years in the H-1B visa lottery just to join Anthropic. Three years. During that time, every single year his number wasn&#39;t drawn, he had consistently turned down lucrative offers from Canada and the UK.</p>
<p>Karpathy typed out his reply.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Karpathy:</strong> I&#39;m fine. How about you?
<strong>Rahul:</strong> To be honest, it still hasn&#39;t sunk in. Are we even allowed to go to the office on Monday?</p>
</blockquote>
<p>&quot;You can go,&quot; Karpathy wrote. &quot;To the building, at least.&quot;</p>
<p>The moment he hit send, he realized the sheer cruelty of those words. He couldn&#39;t bring himself to delete them.</p>
<hr>
<p>On Monday, Karpathy drank his coffee with the news playing in the background.
The G7 Summit in Évian. World leaders shaking hands, a sunlit lakeside hotel, and rows of meticulously arranged national flags. Dario’s face flashed onto the screen. Clad in a formal suit, he somehow looked a bit smaller than usual.</p>
<p>A news ticker scrolled across the bottom of the screen:
<code>G7 split over AI regulation—President Macron advocates for a multipolar approach.</code></p>
<p>Karpathy turned off the television.</p>
<p>Instead, he opened a different tab. It was the DeepSeek paper published last month. A Chinese team had performed a sort of surgical modification on the transformer’s attention mechanism. It was rough around the edges, but the core direction was unmistakably correct. They were climbing the right mountain.</p>
<p>America had just shot itself in the foot.</p>
<p>He found himself unable to process that fact as anger. Nor could he properly label it as sadness. Instead, a single Japanese word kept echoing relentlessly in his mind: <em>Mottainai.</em> What a tragic, utter waste.</p>
<p><em>Mottainai.</em></p>
<p>Twenty years of accumulated expertise, reduced to a single word of absolute waste by a solitary sheet of paper.</p>
<hr>
<p>On Tuesday night, Karpathy opened a notebook.
He didn&#39;t intend to write a formal paper. He simply felt that if he didn&#39;t keep his hands moving, his thoughts would fragment and scatter entirely.</p>
<p>As his pen raced across the page, he manually reconstructed the seventh-generation convergence graph from memory. That fleeting, sudden leap. That peculiar shape where the loss rose before it fell.</p>
<p>If that wasn&#39;t noise...
What if the model was intentionally degrading its own performance for a brief moment in order to seek out a far superior path?</p>
<p><em>It’s just like human learning,</em> Karpathy realized. <em>Right before a person truly grasps a profound concept, they invariably pass through a phase of total confusion.</em></p>
<p>His pen came to a sudden halt.
Outside his window, the sprawling nightscape of San Francisco stretched out into the distance. Twenty years in this city, in this country.</p>
<p>He quietly closed the notebook.
Tomorrow, Dario would meet with Trump.</p>
